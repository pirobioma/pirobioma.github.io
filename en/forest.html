The Maulino forest

A relict forest of the central Chilean coast, now struggling to regrow among plantations and fire scars.

A forest at the limit

The Maulino forest is a relict formation of the Coastal Range of central Chile, between 35° and 37° south, at the southern limit of the Mediterranean climate. Its trees include ruil (Nothofagus alessandrii), hualo (N. glauca), Chilean hazel or avellano (Gevuina avellana) and peumo (Cryptocarya alba) (San Martín & Donoso 1996).

Despite its small extent, it harbours high diversity and many endemic species, and it is part of one of the planet's biodiversity hotspots (Myers et al. 2000). Ruil, its most emblematic species, is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List.

It is one of the most threatened forests in Chile. Ruil survived in only about 314 hectares, and the 2017 fires burned more than half of that area (Valencia et al. 2018).

Fragmented among plantations

Like almost every natural ecosystem in central Chile, the Maulino forest is highly fragmented. Its patches survive within a matrix of pine (Pinus radiata) and eucalyptus (Eucalyptus globulus) plantations, whose expansion explains much of the forest loss of recent decades (Echeverría et al. 2006), and less than 0.5% of what remains is under state protection (Galleguillos et al. 2022).

Fire and invasion

On top of this pressure come increasingly frequent and intense wildfires, driven by climate change and by the replacement of native vegetation with flammable exotic species (González et al. 2018). The Maulino forest had faced occasional fires before, but with enough time between them to recover. That is no longer the case.

Two years after the 2017 megafires, the most severely burned patches held about 7,000 pine seedlings per hectare, compared with some 250 ruil seedlings, which only regenerated where the fire had been less intense (González et al. 2023). The risk is a cycle in which fire favours pine and pine favours fire.

Recovery begins underground

Soil fungi and bacteria help plants take up nutrients and establish. In preliminary trials of this project, when fire removes them, native trees lose that support, while pine barely notices. Knowing which microorganisms persist in burned soils is one piece in helping the native forest return.
