The Pirobioma project

Which microorganisms survive in the soil after a wildfire, and what they can do for the recovery of the forest.

The wildfire problem

Wildfires are becoming more frequent and more intense in central and south-central Chile, driven by the decade-long megadrought, climate change and the expansion of fast-growing exotic tree plantations such as Pinus radiata (González et al. 2018). The Maule Region has repeatedly been the setting of large fires that have burned hundreds of thousands of hectares of native forest and plantations (Bowman et al. 2019).

Fire does not only destroy vegetation: it also deeply transforms the soil, altering its physical structure, its chemistry and, above all, the communities of microorganisms that live in it.

The role of post-fire microorganisms

Soil bacteria and fungi are among the first organisms to respond after a fire. They are key to nutrient cycling, to the decomposition of charcoal and remaining organic matter, to the formation of new fertile soil and to re-establishing symbiotic relationships with the plants that begin to regenerate the forest.

Understanding which microorganisms survive in or recolonise the soil, how they change with time since the fire and how well they can grow on carbon-rich substrates is key to understanding, and eventually promoting, the recovery of these ecosystems. We call this set of microorganisms associated with burned soils the pyrobiome (pirobioma).

What we want to answer

After the 2017 megafires, pine (Pinus radiata), a fire-adapted exotic species, is establishing strongly in the burned patches of the Maulino forest, while native southern beeches such as ruil and hualo regenerate far less (González et al. 2023). In earlier trials we observed that native species perform poorly on burned soil but recover when the microorganisms of the mature forest are returned to it; pine, on the other hand, is barely affected.

The project seeks to understand the role of the soil microbiome in this competition between native and exotic trees after fire. To do so, we aim to:

  • Describe the soil microorganisms of the conserved Maulino forest and of its different post-fire states: burned without vegetation, recolonised by native species, or invaded by pine.
  • Identify how the microorganisms of the original forest help ruil (Nothofagus alessandrii) and hualo (N. glauca) to establish on burned soils.
  • Assess whether soil microorganisms change the outcome of competition between these native trees and pine.
  • Estimate, using spatial information, where these species could establish in the Maule Region under different fire scenarios.

The catalogue of culturable strains is one piece of this work: a living collection of the microorganisms that persist in burned soils.

Where do the soils come from?

The strains in the catalogue come from a paired sampling design in the Maule Region that compares native forest and Pinus radiata plantations under three burn conditions.

ConditionNative forestP. radiata plantation
Unburned (control)Pilén, El Secreto de Pilén ReserveEmpedrado municipality
Recently burned (~1 year)MolcoHuaquén
Burned long ago (~8 years)Los Ruiles ReserveEmpedrado

For now, the catalogue gathers the culturable strains isolated on charcoal agar from the burned soils of this design. As new strains are added, the remaining conditions will be completed.

What could these microorganisms be used for?

  • Restoration of soils degraded by fire, by identifying microorganisms with the potential to accelerate ecosystem recovery.
  • Bioindicators of soil resilience and health, by following how these communities change with time since the fire.
  • Biotechnological potential of strains with particular abilities, such as growing on charcoal substrates, with possible applications in agriculture or bioremediation.
